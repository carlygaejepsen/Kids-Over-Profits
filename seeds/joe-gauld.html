Gauld founded Hyde School in Bath, Maine, in 1966 and led it as founder, headmaster and later president until his death. In 1974 he openly described slapping and paddling students, public self-criticism sessions, forced haircuts and a weight-loss rule tied to grades. Hyde is still run by his family, and in 2025 it was sued in federal court over alleged forced labor and emotional abuse.1, 2, 3

Roles and timeline

  • 1951: Graduated from Bowdoin College (economics); later earned a master's in mathematics from Boston University.1
  • About 1951-1964: Taught and coached at New Hampton School in New Hampshire for 13 years, rising to assistant headmaster.1
  • 1964-1966: Headmaster of Berwick Academy in Maine; received a board no-confidence vote after one year, then left.1
  • 1966: Bought the Hyde mansion in Bath for $160,000 and opened Hyde School with 57 students, many from troubled families or psychiatric hospitals.1
  • 1973: Began a weekly column in the Maine Sunday Telegram.1
  • 1982-1984: Stepped back; a new headmaster tried to move Hyde away from Gauld's personal leadership. The board brought him back as CEO in 1984 with $500,000 in pledged gifts as Hyde ran deficits.1
  • 1987: Appointed his son Malcolm headmaster.1
  • 1989: Hyde was featured on CBS's 60 Minutes; Gauld called the segment "fair and honest."1
  • 1991-1993: Sought $900,000 for a Hyde Training Center to bring the model into public schools; published Character First: The Hyde School Difference (1993).1
  • 2023: Died March 31 in Bath.1

Methods, in his own words

In a 1974 Maine Times interview, Gauld said students were sometimes slapped, publicly paddled and made to attend regular self-criticism seminars. He said he first slapped a student around 1969. Staff once dunked a girl in the campus Duck Pond.2

  • Every new student went through an eight-week summer session that the director compared to boot camp.2
  • Students were encouraged to report on each other. Rule-breakers could get a Marine-style haircut or a work detail.2
  • Students who struggled could be made to live alone and be removed from class.2
  • Heavier students ate at a diet table and failed that week's schoolwork if they did not lose a set amount of weight.2
  • Hyde could ask graduates to return their diplomas if they did not live up to its values.2

In his own newspaper column, Gauld described chasing a 16-year-old applicant around campus during her interview. He wrote that he slapped her three times and threatened to throw her in the pond until she apologized.4

Two early teachers told the Maine Times that Gauld found each person's weakness and used it to control them. The state education commissioner said he questioned the physical roughing-up of students.2

Legal actions and the family enterprise

In 2025, former students filed a federal suit seeking class action status. They allege forced labor and emotional abuse. The defendants include Head of School Laura Gauld, Executive Director Malcolm Gauld and other family members. Hyde denies the allegations.3

The amended complaint adds a section arguing Hyde has always been a profitable Gauld family business. It quotes Gauld's 1973 book A Courage to Grow.5

Influence beyond Hyde

Gauld pushed to export the Hyde model into public education. That effort produced Hyde-branded public schools in New Haven, New York, Orlando and Washington, D.C., plus charter schools in the Bronx and Brooklyn.1

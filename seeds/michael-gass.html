Research draft - not for publication. Gass is profiled for his role as the industry's main research partner. No harm is attributed to him. Funding figures and the trial's status come from public sources cited below.

Gass is a University of New Hampshire professor who directs the Outdoor Behavioral Healthcare Center and NATSAP's research database. The industry's own trade council sponsored the center and funds most of its work. He co-wrote the field's best-selling textbook. His research on safety, outcomes and cost is what wilderness programs cite to parents, insurers and legislators. He is the industry's leading academic validator rather than an operator.1, 2, 4

Roles and timeline

  • Early career: PhD in educational research, University of Colorado Boulder; postdoctoral work in clinical marriage and family therapy.1
  • Undated: Created and directed Project Impact and Family Expedition, two federally funded projects on youth with behavioral problems.2
  • Ongoing: Professor of outdoor education at UNH; director of the Outdoor Behavioral Healthcare Center (OBHC) and of the NATSAP Practice Research Network database.2, 3
  • 2020: Second edition of Adventure Therapy: Theory, Research, and Practice, with Lee Gillis and Keith Russell, billed as the field's best-selling textbook.2
  • February 2021: Won $2.97 million from family foundations for a randomized trial comparing wilderness therapy with community CBT.5
  • Later: The trial (NCT04760938) was terminated. The listed reason: the comparison group failed to provide an adequate comparison.6

Industry funding

The Outdoor Behavioral Healthcare Council, a trade group of 20-plus wilderness programs, sponsored the creation of the OBH Center. The Stories from the Field podcast reported that the center is funded primarily by council members. Gass also runs NATSAP's research database, so both of the field's main trade groups rely on him for outcome data.1, 4

On the same podcast Gass discussed "how researchers address biases" and how the center is funded.1

What the research is used for

  • Safety: The center's 10-year incident-monitoring study compared wilderness injury rates to football and skiing. Programs cite it to argue wilderness therapy is safe.
  • Cost and outcomes: Gass has reported wilderness therapy is cost-effective with good completion rates, and said the goal was to "get it out there." The industry has pushed for insurance coverage, and families have sued insurers to cover wilderness programs.1
  • The randomized trial: Pitched as the first controlled test of effectiveness, it was terminated before producing that answer. Before it began, the UNH student paper reported Gass saying the team already knew wilderness therapy was safe.6, 7

Connections

  • Collaborators: Anita Tucker (OBH Center researcher), Lee Gillis and Keith Russell (co-authors).2
  • Industry bodies: Outdoor Behavioral Healthcare Council and NATSAP. Most programs profiled on this site were members of one or both.

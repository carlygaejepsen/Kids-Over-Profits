Research draft - not for publication. This profile draws on the two full biographies (Pollak 1997, Sutton 1995) and the accounts former students published after Bettelheim's death in 1990. Accounts of abuse are attributed to those who gave them; Bettelheim was never charged, and his defenders disputed them.

Bettelheim was an Austrian-born writer who directed the University of Chicago's Orthogenic School, a residential school for emotionally disturbed children, from 1944 to 1973. His books presented the school's "milieu therapy," in which every part of a child's day was treatment and contact with parents was cut back, as a model for residential care. He blamed cold mothers for autism. After his suicide in 1990, former students and counselors described a director who hit and humiliated children, and biographers showed he had overstated his credentials.1, 2, 3

Roles and timeline

  • 1903: Born in Vienna.1, 2
  • 1938: Received a doctorate from the University of Vienna in philosophy, with a dissertation on aesthetics, not psychology; he had no clinical psychoanalytic training.1
  • 1938-1939: Held in the Dachau and Buchenwald concentration camps after the Anschluss; released in 1939 and emigrated to the United States.1, 2
  • 1943: Published "Individual and Mass Behavior in Extreme Situations," his account of camp prisoners, which made his name.2
  • 1944-1973: Director of the Sonia Shankman Orthogenic School at the University of Chicago.1, 2
  • 1950-1967: Wrote Love Is Not Enough (1950), Truants from Life (1955) and The Empty Fortress (1967), presenting the school's methods and his theory of autism.1, 2
  • 1976-1977: The Uses of Enchantment won the National Book Award.2
  • March 13, 1990: Died by suicide in Silver Spring, Maryland.1, 2
  • 1990 onward: Former students and counselors published accounts of beatings and fear at the school.3, 4

Methods

Bettelheim described the Orthogenic School as a "total therapeutic milieu": children lived at the school for years, every activity was meant to be therapeutic, and parents were kept at a distance because he held them responsible for the child's condition. His critics later called the separation a "parentectomy." He did not hold a clinical license and the school was run on his personal authority.1, 2

In The Empty Fortress he compared autistic children to concentration camp prisoners and argued that autism was a child's response to a mother who did not want them. That "refrigerator mother" idea, which he did not originate but made famous, led a generation of parents to be blamed for their children's autism. It has been abandoned by researchers.1, 2

Accounts of abuse

Weeks after his death, former students began to write publicly about the school. Ronald Angres, a former student, described him as a tyrant who beat children; Charles Pekow, another former student, described being slapped and living in fear of him. Other former students and counselors told his biographers of children struck in front of others, dragged by the hair and humiliated. Some former staff and students defended him.3, 4, 5

Biographers also found that Bettelheim had overstated his training and the school's cure rates, and the folklorist Alan Dundes accused him of taking passages of The Uses of Enchantment from an earlier book by Julius Heuscher.1, 6

Why he matters

Bettelheim was the most famous American voice for removing troubled children from their families and treating them in a closed residential setting. His books reached parents, teachers and clinicians, and lent academic standing to the idea that a total institution could repair a child. The pattern later reported at troubled teen programs, a charismatic director, little outside oversight, restricted family contact and abuse that came out only when former residents spoke as adults, was reported at his school decades earlier.

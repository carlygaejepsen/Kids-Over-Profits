Lester Roloff (1914-1982) was an Independent Fundamentalist Baptist evangelist, radio preacher and founder of Roloff Evangelistic Enterprises. He founded the Rebekah Home for Girls and a network of homes in South Texas, and fought state licensing for about a decade, calling the standoff the "Christian Alamo." His homes became the model for IFB reform schools, and his "Bible discipline" lineage runs through programs such as Agape and Circle of Hope.1, 3, 5

Roles and timeline

  • 1944: Began broadcasting The Family Altar.1, 2
  • 1951: Set up Roloff Evangelistic Enterprises in Corpus Christi, with a daily radio program.3
  • 1958: The ASU dissertation dates the Lighthouse for Boys to 1958.3
  • 1967: Founded People's Baptist Church in Corpus Christi.3
  • 1967 or 1968: Founded the Rebekah Home for Girls; sources disagree on the year.3, 5
  • 1973: Parents reported seeing a girl whipped. Attorney General John Hill sued with affidavits from 16 girls describing whippings with leather straps, paddling, handcuffing to drainpipes and isolation cells.3, 5
  • 1975: Texas passed a youth home licensing law; Roloff was arrested twice for refusing to comply.5
  • 1979: The Rebekah and Lighthouse homes were shut down, then reopened under People's Baptist Church.4, 5
  • 1981: A state district judge ruled the homes exempt from licensing as part of the church.9
  • 1982: Died November 2 piloting his own plane near Normangee, Texas, along with four women from his ministry.1
  • 1984-1985: Texas prevailed in court and the Rebekah Home closed in Texas. The dissertation says Roloff's successors moved roughly 100 children to Missouri rather than comply.3, 5, 6

Methods

Roloff called his discipline "Bible discipline," grounded in scripture about the rod. Girls described being punished for failing to memorize a Bible verse or forgetting to make a bed. Other accounts describe girls bound with duct tape and locked in isolation cells while Roloff's sermons played over speakers. Attribute each practice to the particular witness, report or home rather than assuming identical practices at every Roloff home.3, 4, 5

Daily church attendance was mandatory, and each home had its own church and pastor on the grounds. Roloff advertised the homes on the radio as a place for girls from "jail houses, broken homes, hippie hives, and dope dives."5

The licensing fight

Roloff's defense was that the homes were church ministries, so the state had no authority over them. When pressed, he moved children to homes in Georgia, Mississippi and Missouri, including New Bethany, and brought them back when rulings went his way.3, 5

That church-ministry argument became the standard legal shield for IFB programs. It is a direct ancestor of the religious exemptions that let Missouri programs operate unlicensed until 2021. Cite the actual opinions and orders before describing their holdings in detail.3, 6

After his death

Wiley Cameron, his right-hand man, succeeded him as head of the Rebekah Home. The dissertation describes Texas policy changes under Governor George W. Bush in the late 1990s that let the homes return under private accreditation, followed by new abuse allegations; Cameron closed the Rebekah Home in 2001. Do not infer that Bush intended abuse or personally directed the homes.3, 5, 7

Connections

  • Programs he founded: Rebekah Home for Girls, Bethesda Home for Girls, Anchor Home for Boys, Lighthouse for Boys, People's Baptist Church and Roloff Evangelistic Enterprises.
  • Descendant programs: Agape Boarding School displayed his photo on campus. The Roloff frame on the network map also holds the Clemensens (Agape), Boyd and Stephanie Householder (Circle of Hope) and Berean Baptist Church.
  • New Bethany: Thelma Ford of New Bethany testified in 1997 that Rebekah girls were boarded there during the Texas fight.
  • Herman Fountain: Worked at Rebekah Home in 1977 before establishing Bethel Baptist Home for Children in Mississippi. The ASU dissertation treats this as a transfer of Roloff's model; shared lineage does not by itself establish that Roloff directed Fountain's later operations.3, 8
